Choosing ERP for private label manufacturers is different from choosing ERP for a plant that makes a single brand. A private label manufacturer makes products that carry other companies' names, such as retailers, distributors and brand owners. Each customer brings its own specifications, item numbers, labels, packaging and audit expectations, and the same base product can ship as several different customer items in the same week.

This checklist sets out ten requirements to test any ERP system against, whether you are replacing spreadsheets or outgrowing your current system. Use it before vendor demos, so that each vendor answers your questions instead of following its standard script. For background on the private label industry, see our [PLMA 2026 guide for private label manufacturers](/plma-2026).

## Why private label manufacturers need ERP built around their customers

When you make your own brand, you set one standard for each product. In private label manufacturing, each customer sets its own standard, and you have to prove you meet it.

Retailers' supplier manuals show how detailed these requirements can be. [7-Eleven's vendor and supplier guidelines](https://www.7-eleven.com/legal/vendor-supplier-guidelines) (last updated June 1, 2026) ask suppliers of its own-brand products to use a clearly defined lot or batch identification system, to print lot codes on the smallest pack size and to provide accurate formula and ingredient information for each product. [The Hershey Company's Supplier Quality Expectations Manual](https://www.thehersheycompany.com/content/dam/hersheyterms/supplier-code-of-conduct/supplier-quality-expectations-manual-english.pdf) (August 11, 2025) asks its suppliers to document lot tracking from receipt through usage, keep Certificates of Analysis for incoming materials and get changes approved before they are made.

These are individual companies' requirements, not universal rules, but each one becomes a record that your ERP has to hold.

Food manufacturers also need to watch regulation. The FDA's [Food Traceability Rule (FSMA 204)](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-safety-modernization-act-fsma/fsma-final-rule-requirements-additional-traceability-records-certain-foods) adds record-keeping requirements for foods on its Food Traceability List: Key Data Elements recorded at Critical Tracking Events, a written traceability plan and, when FDA needs it, an electronic sortable spreadsheet within 24 hours of a request. FDA has said it will not enforce the rule before July 20, 2028, following a directive from Congress. If any of your products or ingredients are on the list, plan your ERP data for it now.

## The ERP for private label manufacturers checklist: 10 requirements

| # | Requirement | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Customer-specific product specifications | One base product linked to each customer's version |
| 2 | Formulation and recipe management | Versioned formulas with approvals and a change history |
| 3 | Lot traceability, backward and forward | From supplier lots to customer shipments in one search |
| 4 | Quality control and audit documents | Holds, releases and certificates attached to each lot |
| 5 | Inventory of materials, packaging and finished goods | Customer-specific packaging tracked as its own stock |
| 6 | Production planning and scheduling | Plans built from confirmed orders, with changeovers in view |
| 7 | Customer-specific order management | Customer item numbers, prices and shipping rules |
| 8 | Costing and margin by customer and SKU | Actual batch costs, not only standard costs |
| 9 | Integrations and reporting | Accounting, EDI and plant systems connected |
| 10 | Custom modules and scalability | A way to add processes without replacing the system |

### 1. Customer-specific product specifications

The same base product often ships as several customer items, each with its own item number, label, artwork, pack size and approved specification. Your ERP should link one base product to every customer version, keep specification and artwork versions with an approval status, and show the right customer item number on orders, pick lists and invoices.

**Ask the vendor:** "Show us one base product sold to two customers with different specifications and labels."

### 2. Formulation and recipe management

Look for versioned formulas, an approval step before a new version is used and a history of who changed what and when. 7-Eleven, for example, asks suppliers to keep records documenting formula and ingredient changes.

**Ask the vendor:** "How is a formula change approved, and where can we see the full change history?"

### 3. Lot traceability, backward and forward

This is the requirement customers and auditors are most likely to test. Your ERP should record supplier lots when materials arrive, the material lots used in every batch, and every customer shipment of each finished lot. From one finished lot, you should be able to see every raw material and packaging lot that went into it and every customer that received it.

**Ask the vendor:** "Start from one finished lot and show us its materials and its shipments while we watch."

### 4. Quality control and audit documentation

Look for incoming inspections, Certificates of Analysis stored against the material lot, holds that stop a lot being used or shipped, release records and non-conformance reports. Documents should be attached to the lot, so that you are not searching shared drives during an audit.

**Ask the vendor:** "How does a lot go on hold, and what stops someone shipping it?"

### 5. Inventory of raw materials, packaging and finished goods

Shared ingredients can be used for any customer, but printed packaging and labels usually belong to one customer. When a customer changes artwork or drops an item, that packaging can become obsolete. Your ERP should track customer-specific packaging as separate stock, record expiry or best-by dates and support first-expired, first-out picking where you need it.

**Ask the vendor:** "Which packaging can only be used for one customer, and what is it worth today?"

### 6. Production planning and scheduling

Orders from different customers compete for the same lines, and every changeover between customer packs takes time. Look for work orders created from confirmed orders and forecasts, checks on material availability before a batch is scheduled, visible changeovers and recorded actual yields.

**Ask the vendor:** "What happens to the plan when a customer brings an order forward by a week?"

### 7. Customer-specific order management

Each customer has its own item numbers, price lists, pack configurations, delivery addresses and labeling rules, and larger customers often send orders electronically. Your ERP should hold these rules per customer, so that orders are not re-keyed and shipping documents match what each customer expects.

**Ask the vendor:** "Can orders from our largest customer arrive electronically, and what happens when one fails?"

### 8. Costing and margin by customer and SKU

Two customer items made from the same base product can have very different margins once packaging, changeovers and yields are included. Look for actual costs per batch (materials, packaging, labor and yield) and margin reports by customer and by customer item.

**Ask the vendor:** "Show the margin on one customer item using last month's actual batch costs."

### 9. Integrations and reporting

List the systems your ERP has to exchange data with, such as accounting, EDI, warehouse systems, scales, label printers and quality systems, and check which connections are standard and which need custom work. Reports should answer daily questions without exporting everything to spreadsheets.

**Ask the vendor:** "Which of our integrations are standard, which are custom, and who maintains them after upgrades?"

### 10. Custom modules and scalability

New customers bring new requirements. Check how the system handles a process it does not cover today: configuration, an add-on, a custom module or a workaround outside the system. Also confirm who owns your data and how you can export it.

**Ask the vendor:** "How would we add a process your standard product does not cover, and what does it cost to maintain?"

## How to evaluate an ERP system for private label manufacturing

1. **Collect your customers' requirements.** Supplier manuals, specifications and recent audit findings are your most reliable requirements list.
2. **Map your current process.** Follow one order from material receipt to customer shipment, and mark every place where data is re-keyed or kept in a spreadsheet.
3. **Rank the ten requirements.** Mark each one as must-have, important or nice to have for your plant.
4. **Run scripted demos with your own data.** Give each vendor one real product, two customer versions of it and one trace exercise, and score every vendor on the same script.
5. **Speak to similar manufacturers.** Ask for references from plants with processes like yours.
6. **Compare the total cost.** Include licenses or subscriptions, implementation, integrations, customization, training and ongoing support, not just the first-year price.

## Packaged ERP or custom ERP modules?

| | Packaged ERP | Custom ERP modules |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best when | Your processes are close to industry standard | Your process is a competitive advantage or a customer requirement the package does not cover |
| Strengths | Ready-made features, vendor-maintained updates | Fits how your plant actually works |
| Trade-offs | You adapt your process to the software; heavy customization can complicate upgrades | Needs clear requirements, testing and a long-term development partner |

Many manufacturers combine the two: standard functions such as accounting on a packaged system, and custom modules where their process is different, such as customer specification management.

## How to choose the right ERP

Shortlist no more than three options, score them on the same scripted demo and check the gaps against your must-have list. Plan a phased rollout that starts where the risk or manual work is highest, often traceability, specifications or order entry. Agree how you will measure success before you start, for example the time a trace exercise takes or the number of order-entry corrections each month.

## Conclusion

The right ERP for private label manufacturers can prove, customer by customer, that every product was made to the agreed specification from traceable materials, at a margin you understand.
