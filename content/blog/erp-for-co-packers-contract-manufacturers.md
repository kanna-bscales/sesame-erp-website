Choosing ERP for co-packers is harder than it looks, because a co-packer does not run one business. It runs a slightly different business for every customer. Each brand owner sends its own formulas or bills of materials, its own packaging, sometimes its own raw materials, and its own delivery dates, and all of them compete for the same lines and the same people.

This guide explains what co-packers and contract manufacturers need from an ERP system, how the co-packing workflow should run through it, and the questions to ask before you choose software or add custom modules. If you make retailer-brand products as well, read our [ERP checklist for private label manufacturers](/blog/erp-for-private-label-manufacturers-checklist), which covers retailer specifications and audits in more depth.

## What is a co-packer?

A co-packer (contract packer) manufactures, packs or both for other companies, which sell the finished product under their own brand. A contract manufacturer does the same for production in general. In practice the terms overlap, and many plants do both. The common thread is that the brand owner sets the requirements and you make the product, so your records have to show, customer by customer, what you made, from which materials, to which specification.

Co-packing arrangements usually take one of two forms, and many plants run both at once:

- **Turnkey:** you buy the ingredients and packaging, make the product and invoice the finished goods.
- **Toll or consigned:** the customer supplies some or all materials, which usually remain the customer's property under the agreement, and you charge for the work.

## Why co-packers need ERP software

Spreadsheets and entry-level systems work while you have a few customers. They start to fail when the same people have to answer, at the same time, questions such as:

- Whose stock is this pallet, and can we use it for another customer's order?
- Which lines are available next week after this customer moved an order forward?
- What did the last run of this item actually cost, including the changeover?
- Which finished lots used this supplier lot, and which customers received them?

Your customers also pass their own requirements down to you. For example, the Proprietary Product Suppliers Addendum in 7-Eleven's official [Vendor/Supplier Guidelines](https://www.7-eleven.com/legal/vendor-supplier-guidelines), which covers suppliers of 7-Eleven's own-brand (proprietary) products, requires those suppliers to keep records documenting their use of co-packers or third-party manufacturers and to maintain current lists of their ingredient suppliers. A brand owner can only meet requirements like these if its co-packer can produce the records quickly.

For food, the FDA's [Food Traceability Rule (FSMA 204)](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-safety-modernization-act-fsma/fsma-final-rule-requirements-additional-traceability-records-certain-foods) adds record-keeping requirements for businesses that manufacture, process, pack or hold foods on its Food Traceability List, unless an exemption applies. It does not cover every co-packer: whether it applies to you depends on the foods you handle. FDA has said it will not enforce the rule before July 20, 2028, following a directive from Congress.

## ERP for co-packers: 8 important features

### 1. Customer management

Every brand owner has its own terms, contacts, approval steps and document requirements. Your ERP should hold these per customer, so that whoever takes an order or schedules a run sees that customer's rules without searching email.

**Example:** one customer requires a certificate with every shipment and another requires sample retention for each lot. Both rules sit on the customer record and appear on the right shipments automatically.

### 2. Customer-specific products and bills of materials

The same base product can be packed for several customers, each with its own item number, formula or bill of materials, packaging, labels and pack size. Look for customer-specific items linked to a shared base where one exists, versioned bills of materials and approvals before a new version is used.

**Example:** a customer changes its film supplier. The new bill of materials is approved for that customer only, and the old film stays available until it is used up.

### 3. Inventory: customer-owned and company-owned stock

This is where many systems fall short. Materials a customer supplies usually remain the customer's property under your agreement, so they must not be used for another customer's order, and the customer will expect regular stock reports and reconciliation. Your ERP should record the owner of every lot, keep customer-owned stock separate, show usage and yield by run, and report balances per customer. Ownership tracking also gives your finance team the information it needs to account for customer-owned materials in line with your agreements and your accountant's advice.

**Example:** a customer asks why 40 cases' worth of its packaging is missing. You can show which runs used it, the recorded waste and the remaining balance, lot by lot.

### 4. Production scheduling on shared lines

Orders from different customers compete for the same lines, and the order of runs matters: allergen changeovers, cleaning, packaging format changes and labor all depend on it. Look for scheduling that shows every customer's runs on one plan, checks material availability (including customer-supplied materials that have not arrived yet) and makes changeovers visible.

**Example:** a customer brings an order forward by a week. The planner can see which runs move, which materials are short and which customers need to be told.

### 5. Lot and batch traceability

Record supplier and customer-supplied lots on receipt, the material lots consumed by each batch, and every shipment of each finished lot. One search should show everything a finished lot contains and everywhere it went, across all customers.

**Example:** a supplier warns about one lot of an ingredient used by three customers. You can list the affected finished lots and shipments for each customer in minutes, not days.

### 6. Quality management

Inspections on receipt, in-process checks, holds, release records and non-conformances should be recorded against the lot and the run, with documents attached. Holds must actually stop a lot from being used or shipped.

**Example:** an in-process weight check fails. The run's output goes on hold automatically, and the quality team releases or rejects it with a recorded reason.

### 7. Quoting, costing and order management

Co-packing margins depend on accurate quotes and on knowing what each run really cost. Look for quotes built from materials, packaging, line time, labor, changeovers and expected yield. Actual costs should be recorded per run, so that you can compare quote and actual by customer and item. Orders should carry each customer's item numbers, prices, delivery rules and, where used, electronic order exchange.

**Example:** a customer asks for a price on a new pack size. You quote from the last three runs' actual yields and line times, not from estimates in a spreadsheet.

### 8. Reporting and automation

Managers need order status, inventory by owner, schedule adherence, yields and margins without exporting everything to spreadsheets. Routine work, such as stock reports to customers, low-material alerts and order confirmations, should run automatically.

**Example:** every Monday each customer receives a report of its stock held at your plant, generated from the ERP rather than typed up by hand.

## How ERP manages the co-packing workflow

1. **Quote:** the customer requests a price; you quote from materials, line time and expected yield.
2. **Set up:** the item, specification, bill of materials and packaging are approved for that customer.
3. **Order:** the customer's order arrives, by email, portal or electronic exchange, with its own item numbers.
4. **Materials:** you buy your materials and receive customer-supplied materials, each lot recorded with its owner.
5. **Schedule:** the run is placed on a shared line, with changeovers and material availability checked.
6. **Produce:** materials are consumed against the batch, with quality checks and actual yields recorded.
7. **Release and ship:** quality releases the lot; the shipment records which lots went to which location.
8. **Invoice and report:** you invoice the work or finished goods, compare actual cost with the quote and report customer stock balances.

## Common problems without a connected ERP

- Customer-owned and company-owned stock mixed together, discovered only at stock counts.
- Schedules on whiteboards or spreadsheets that are out of date as soon as an order changes.
- Quotes based on estimates that are never compared with actual runs.
- Lot records split across paper batch sheets, spreadsheets and email, so a trace takes days.
- The same order typed into several systems, with errors at each step.

## How to choose ERP for contract manufacturing

- **Customer-owned stock:** ask the vendor to show a customer-supplied material received, consumed in a run and reported back to that customer.
- **Scheduling:** give each vendor one realistic week of your orders across several customers, including an allergen changeover.
- **Costing:** ask to see quote against actual cost for one run, including yield and changeover time.
- **Traceability:** start from one supplier lot used by two customers and ask for the full trace.
- **Integrations:** check which connections to your customers' order systems, accounting and plant equipment are standard and which need custom work.
- **References:** speak to plants with a similar mix of turnkey and toll work.

## When should a co-packer consider custom ERP modules?

Packaged ERP covers common manufacturing processes well. Custom modules make sense where your business is different from the standard: for example, customer-specific stock reporting, a quoting model built on your own line-time data, or a customer portal for orders and stock balances. Many co-packers keep accounting and standard functions on packaged software and build custom modules only where the package forces workarounds.

Before building, write down the process, the people who use it and the reports it must produce, and agree who will maintain the module after go-live.

## Conclusion

A co-packer's ERP has to keep many customers' products, materials, schedules and records separate while running them through one plant. If your system can show who owns every lot, what every run really cost and where every finished lot went, you can quote with confidence and answer customers' questions quickly.
