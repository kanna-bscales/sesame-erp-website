Every manufacturer choosing a new system faces the same question sooner or later: packaged ERP vs custom ERP. Do you buy a ready-made product and adapt to it, or build software around how you work? The honest answer is that it is rarely one or the other. Most businesses are best served by deciding process by process, and that is what this guide helps you do.

If you are a private label manufacturer, our [ERP checklist for private label manufacturers](/blog/erp-for-private-label-manufacturers-checklist) lists the ten requirements to test first. This article is about the decision that comes after: which of those requirements a package should cover, and which deserve a custom module.

## What is packaged ERP?

Packaged ERP (also called off-the-shelf ERP) is software built once by a vendor and sold to many companies. You configure it with settings, workflows and reports, but the core design is the vendor's. Updates arrive on the vendor's schedule, and the same product serves many businesses in your industry.

That shared design is both the strength and the limit. The package contains years of other companies' experience, so standard work is covered on the first day. It also means the software was designed for a typical business, and the parts of your plant that are not typical have to be handled some other way.

## What are custom ERP modules?

A custom ERP module is software built for one business's process, such as customer specification management, a quoting model based on your own line data, or a customer portal. It can run alongside packaged software or form part of a fully custom system. You decide how it works, and you are responsible, with your development partner, for maintaining it.

Custom does not have to mean building everything. A module covers one process from start to finish, with its own screens, rules and reports, and exchanges data with the rest of your systems.

## Packaged ERP vs custom ERP at a glance

| Question | Packaged ERP | Custom ERP modules |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fit | Built for a typical business in your industry | Built for how your business works |
| Speed to start | Fast for standard processes | Depends on scope; start with one module |
| Updates | Vendor delivers them; heavy customization can make them harder | You plan them with your development partner |
| Process change | You often adapt your process to the software | The software follows your process |
| Main risk | Workarounds and spreadsheets around the gaps | Unclear requirements, or no one to maintain it |
| Best for | Accounting, payroll, standard purchasing and sales | Processes that set you apart or that customers require |

## When packaged ERP is the right choice

- **Your process is standard.** General ledger, payables and payroll work the same way in most companies. There is little to gain from building them again.
- **Regulation or reporting is built in.** Tax, payroll and financial reporting change often, and a vendor that serves many customers keeps up with them.
- **You want to follow industry practice.** If the package's way of working is better than yours, adopting it is an improvement, not a compromise.
- **You have no one to own software.** A custom module needs someone in your business who decides how it should work. If nobody can take that role, a package is the safer choice.

## When custom ERP modules make more sense

- **Your process is your advantage.** If customers choose you for how fast you quote, how you handle their materials or how you report to them, forcing that process into a package can remove the reason they buy.
- **Customers require it.** Private label and co-packing customers often bring their own specifications, documents and reports. Our guide to [ERP for co-packers](/blog/erp-for-co-packers-contract-manufacturers) shows how customer-owned stock and quote-versus-actual costing often fall outside standard software.
- **The workarounds have become the system.** When the real work happens in spreadsheets around the ERP, the package is not covering the process, whatever the feature list says.
- **The package can do it, but only with heavy customization.** A package changed far beyond its design is harder to upgrade and still does not belong to you.

## The hybrid approach: package for the standard, modules for the rest

Many manufacturers combine the two. Accounting and other standard functions stay on packaged software, and custom modules cover the processes where the package forces workarounds. The modules exchange data with the package, so finance still has one set of books.

The hybrid approach also lowers risk. Instead of replacing everything at once, you start with the single module that removes the most manual work, prove it, and then decide on the next one.

It does add one thing to manage: the connection between the systems. Agree at the start which system holds each record, such as items, customers, prices and stock, and how changes travel from one to the other. Most problems in a hybrid setup come from two systems each believing they own the same data.

## How to decide, process by process

1. **List your core processes**, from quote to cash and from purchase to production.
2. **Mark each one standard or different.** Standard means most companies in your industry do it the same way. Different means your customers, products or methods make it unusual.
3. **Mark where the workarounds are.** Every spreadsheet, re-keyed order or paper form is a sign of a gap.
4. **Ask what a gap costs.** Time, errors, lost orders or failed audits. Rank the gaps by that cost.
5. **Match the approach to each process.** Standard and no workarounds: packaged. Different, with costly workarounds: a candidate for a custom module.
6. **Plan who maintains what.** For a package, check the vendor's update and support terms. For a module, agree who will maintain it after go-live before you build it.

## A worked example

This example is illustrative, not a real customer. A plant makes sauces for four retail customers and packs for two brand owners that supply their own packaging.

| Process | Standard or different? | Workarounds today | Approach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accounting and payroll | Standard | None | Packaged |
| Purchasing | Standard | None | Packaged |
| Customer specifications and labels | Different for every customer | Shared folders and email | Custom module candidate |
| Customer-supplied packaging stock | Different | A spreadsheet per customer | Custom module candidate |
| Quoting new pack sizes | Different | Estimates in a spreadsheet | Custom module candidate, after the first two |
| Production scheduling | Partly standard | A whiteboard | Test the package first |

The result is not "packaged" or "custom". It is a short list: keep the package for finance and purchasing, test whether its scheduling is good enough, and build first where the workarounds cost the most. In this example that is customer-supplied stock, because mistakes there are visible to customers.

## Questions to ask before you choose

- Which of our processes would we have to change to fit this package, and are we happy to change them?
- How are customizations handled when the package is upgraded?
- For custom modules: who owns the code and the data, and how do we export it?
- How will the package and the modules share data, and who maintains that connection?
- What is the total cost over several years, including implementation, integrations, training and support, not only the first-year price?
- Who in our business will own each process and decide how the software should handle it?

## Common mistakes

- **Customizing a package until it becomes custom software** without the benefits of either.
- **Building custom software for standard processes** such as accounting, where a package does the job well.
- **Choosing on the demo script** instead of on your own products and your own week of orders.
- **Building without a maintenance plan**, so the module ages as soon as the developer leaves.
- **Deciding everything at once.** A decision made process by process can be corrected; a single decision for the whole business is much harder to reverse.

## Where AI agents fit

AI agents are software that completes a routine task on its own, such as drafting an order from an email or preparing a stock report, and hands the result to a person to approve. They can be added to either approach, but they work best where the data and the process are well defined, which is often a custom module built around exactly that process.

## Conclusion

Packaged ERP vs custom ERP is not a single decision. Use packaged software where your processes are standard and custom modules where your process, or your customers' requirements, set you apart. Decide process by process, start where the workarounds cost the most, and agree how everything will be maintained before you begin.
